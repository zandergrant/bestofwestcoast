Oregon · Cannon Beach

Best things to do in Cannon Beach

The rock at low tide, the bluff at Ecola, and a town you can walk. The park road is the part that needs a car.

Cannon Beach is a small town on the north Oregon coast, and the trip is the beach, Haystack Rock, and Ecola State Park. The town is walkable once you are there. The park is a short, awkward drive at the north end, on a road that is a bad idea in a big rig. Plan the tide for the rock, and plan the car for the park.

The beach and Haystack Rock

The visitor bureau describes nearly four miles of beach running out from downtown, with Haystack Rock at the edge of the shore. The rock is a basalt sea stack the bureau measures at 235 feet. It is part of the Oregon Islands National Wildlife Refuge above the high tide line, and the intertidal area is a protected marine garden.

At low tide you can walk out to the tidepools. The bureau says the useful window is an hour or more before low tide, and it tells people not to turn their backs on the ocean. Sea stars, anemones, and other animals are the point of that walk. Nothing may be removed from within 300 yards of the rock, and climbing above the barnacle line is prohibited, both to protect the animals and the nesting birds. Tufted puffins are on the rock from early spring to mid-summer. Other birds are there through the year.

Suits almost everyone who came to Cannon Beach, especially anyone who wants wildlife without a hike. Trade-off: it only works at the right tide, and in summer you are sharing the sand with a lot of other people. A midday visit at high tide is a view of the rock, not the pools.

Ecola State Park

Oregon State Parks describes Ecola as wrapping Tillamook Head between Seaside and Cannon Beach, with about nine miles of coastline. The entrance road goes through Sitka spruce forest and opens onto a bluff looking south over sea stacks, the town, and the coastal ridge. Trails look down on coves and on Tillamook Rock Lighthouse, offshore on its own rock. Indian Beach, inside the park, is the sandy cove surfers use, and it has tidepools.

The park’s trail system includes an eight-mile piece of the Oregon Coast Trail and the 2.5-mile Clatsop Loop, which follows part of the route William Clark’s party walked in 1806. As of the park’s notice effective April 17, 2026, the west section of the Clatsop Loop is closed for an active landslide. Check the park page before you count on that loop. The headland has a long history of slides, which is also why roads and trails close for repairs.

From the north end of town, the visitor bureau’s directions are: from 5th Street, north on Ecola Park Road 1.5 miles to the fee station, then left down to Ecola Point or right for two miles to Indian Beach. A day-use permit is required to park. Oregon State Parks says to call for the fee rather than printing a single price, and the bureau says in-state and out-of-state fees differ. An annual Oregon State Parks permit or a Pacific Coast Passport also works. Parking is limited, and from June through September the bureau says lines form. Overnight parking is prohibited. Motorhomes and trucks pulling trailers are not recommended; the road is tight and has little room to turn around. Pets in the park must be on a leash no longer than six feet. Drones are not allowed.

State parks notes that gray whales pass in winter and spring, and that elk and eagles are part of the wildlife you might see. That is a reason to bring binoculars, not a guarantee.

Suits anyone who wants the view down onto Cannon Beach, a picnic, or a real hike. Trade-off: you need a car or a willingness to deal with that narrow road, you will pay to park, and a summer afternoon can be spent in a line. It is a poor plan for an RV. It is also a poor plan if you wanted to stay on the sand all day. Do the rock at the tide you came for, and the park at a different part of the day.

The town itself

The visitor bureau describes Cannon Beach as a small town you can do mostly on foot: leave the car in one spot and walk to the beach, shops, and restaurants. Parking in town is limited and free. The town is about three miles end to end, which is walkable in pieces and long if you try to do every block plus the beach plus the park.

Restaurants run from seated dining to casual rooms, and the bureau says to call for a reservation. The town is busy in summer and on event weekends, and some kitchens keep limited hours even in the quieter months. This guide does not list restaurants. The practical point is to book the one dinner you care about, and not to assume a Monday night in November will have the same hours as August.

Suits a slow afternoon after the beach. Trade-off: the shops are the town, not a second destination. If you only have a few hours, spend them on the rock and the bluff, not in stores.

Getting there, and what to pack

The visitor bureau’s getting-here page puts Cannon Beach less than two hours from Portland, about 80 miles: Highway 26 west to US 101, then four miles south. From Seattle the same page describes I-5 south to Longview, Highway 30 west to Astoria, then 25 miles south on US 101, just under four hours.

You can arrive without a car. Northwest POINT is listed as a twice-daily bus from Portland’s Greyhound and Amtrak stations, with tickets through those companies. Sunset Empire Transportation District runs local buses on the north coast, and it connects with other coastal counties through the North by Northwest Connector. That alliance’s page, as linked by the bureau, lists a three-day pass at $25 and a seven-day pass at $30, sold by drivers for cash and exact change, covering a ride to the coast, the return, and unlimited rides in Clatsop, Tillamook, and Lincoln counties. Confirm the fare and the POINT schedule before you build the day around a bus. The passes were listed when this page was checked; they are not a promise.

A car is still the straightforward way to Ecola, and to anywhere south along 101. Without one, the walkable part is the town and the beach.

The bureau describes a cool marine climate: summer highs in the upper 60s, winter lows in the high 30s, rarely freezing, and the most rain from November through January, often as drizzle. Bring layers and shoes that can take sand and a wet path. A warm afternoon in Portland does not mean a warm afternoon on this beach.
