Washington · Seattle

Best things to do in Seattle

The market, the waterfront, and Seattle Center are one part of town. The ferry and the locks are a decision to leave it.

Seattle’s first-trip sights sit in a line from the market to the waterfront to Seattle Center, with the airport train underneath them. The locks, the ferry, and a viewpoint on Queen Anne are the pieces that ask you to leave that line. Do the line on one day. Leave town, on purpose, on the next.

Pike Place Market

Pike Place Market is a public market at 85 Pike Street. The market’s history page dates the city ordinance to August 5, 1907, and opening day to August 17, when six to twelve farmers sold out by lunch. A 2026 note on the market’s site calls the historic district nine acres, with a mix of farmers, shops, and places to eat. The market also posts seasonal rules for street access. Read those before you assume cars and crowds will be arranged the way they were on your last visit. Hours are on the market’s site; they are not repeated here.

Suits a first morning, and anyone who wants food and a public room rather than a ticketed attraction. Trade-off: it is crowded in the middle of the day, and it is not a quiet neighborhood market. Go early if you want the stalls more than the spectacle.

The waterfront and the sculpture park

Olympic Sculpture Park, part of the Seattle Art Museum, is at 2901 Western Avenue, one mile north of the museum downtown. It is free and open every day, from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset. The art is outdoors on the waterfront, looking over the Salish Sea. Do not climb on the sculptures. Bikes and skateboards get walked, not ridden. Pets are allowed on a leash of six feet or less, except inside the pavilion. The park garage is paid; the visit page lists the rates and says there is no overnight parking.

Suits a walk after the market, in any season, because there is no ticket and no closing hour in the middle of the afternoon. Trade-off: it is a mile of waterfront, not a two-minute stroll from the market sign. The garage bill is annoying if you drove. Walk, or ride, and skip the garage.

Seattle Center

Seattle Center’s address is 305 Harrison Street. Visit Seattle sends people there for the Space Needle, the Museum of Pop Culture, and Chihuly Garden and Glass, and for performances at McCaw Hall and Climate Pledge Arena. Those are separate tickets. The Space Needle, at 400 Broad Street, posts hours that change by day and by season. On October 28, 2026 its site listed general admission as a range, $35 to $55, and said to check closures. Use the live page. Do not treat a saved range as today’s price.

Visit Seattle also says you can walk or take the monorail to Seattle Center. Confirm the monorail’s hours on its own site; this guide did not recheck that operator’s schedule.

Suits the day you are willing to buy tickets, and a rainy day if the museum is the draw. Trade-off: the Needle, Chihuly, and MoPOP will eat a day and a budget if you do all three. Pick one ticketed stop. The grounds themselves are the free part. Kerry Park, up on Queen Anne, is the free view Visit Seattle points to. The trade-off there is the hill.

A ferry, if you want out on the water

Washington State Ferries runs a Seattle–Bainbridge Island route and a Seattle–Bremerton route. Schedules and fares are on the WSDOT ferries site, and they change with the season. A walk-on passenger does not need the car reservation system that drivers deal with. Check the sailing you want the day you go. This guide does not print a departure list, because that list is a different table every season.

Suits a half day when the weather is clear enough that the ride is the point, and anyone who does not want another museum. Trade-off: you are on a boat schedule. Miss the return you planned and the evening moves. Bainbridge is the simpler walk-off; Bremerton is the longer ride. Look at the schedule before you decide which one is actually shorter today.

Ballard Locks, if you are already north

The Hiram M. Chittenden Locks, also called the Ballard Locks, sit at 3015 NW 54th Street. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers finished the Lake Washington Ship Canal and the locks in 1917. They hold Lake Washington and Lake Union above Puget Sound, keep salt water out of the lakes, and move boats between the two levels. There is a fish ladder. The grounds are open 7 a.m. to 9 p.m. The fish-ladder viewing room is open 7 a.m. to 8:45 p.m. Walking the grounds is free. The visitor center’s hours were posted in more than one version on the same page when this was checked, so use the Corps page for the building and trust the grounds hours above.

The north lot is city-owned. The Corps lists parking at $2 an hour, four hours maximum. From May through September, pay parking runs 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. Monday through Saturday. From October through April it runs 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. Monday through Saturday. Sundays and holidays are free.

The Sound Transit 1 Line, the light rail from Lynnwood through downtown to Federal Way, stops at SeaTac Airport, Capitol Hill, Westlake, and the stadiums. It does not stop in Ballard. The locks are a trip, not a stroll from the market.

Suits a Ballard day, or anyone who would rather watch boats and fish than buy a Needle ticket. Trade-off: the time to get there. Do not tack it onto a Seattle Center day.

The train from the airport

Sound Transit’s 1 Line runs from Lynnwood City Center to Federal Way Downtown and stops at SeaTac/Airport, Capitol Hill, Westlake, Pioneer Square, the International District, and Stadium, among others. That is the car-free spine: airport, downtown, Capitol Hill, and the stadiums. Queen Anne, Ballard, and Fremont are off it. Use a trip planner for those.
